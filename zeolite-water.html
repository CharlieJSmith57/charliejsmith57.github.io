Water from air, Abalessa

A research plan for delivering drinking water to a town in the Algerian Sahara with passive, off-grid zeolite atmospheric water generation: no electricity, no refrigeration, just the difference between night and day.

Overview

WPI’s Great Problems Seminar puts first-year students on a real global problem with real constraints. Our team took on drinking water for Abalessa, a remote town in the Tamanrasset Province of southern Algeria, deep in the Sahara. Extreme heat, near-zero rainfall and thin infrastructure make trucked water and wells unreliable or unaffordable, so the question became: what could a community run itself, with no grid?

The technology

Our research centred on zeolite-based atmospheric water generation, a technology developed by researchers at MIT. Zeolites are microporous aluminosilicate minerals with an enormous internal surface area and strong hygroscopic behaviour, so they pull water vapour out of ambient air even at the low humidity of a desert night.

Conventional atmospheric water generators condense moisture with electricity and refrigeration. A zeolite collector needs neither. During the cooler night the material adsorbs humidity; in the heat of the day it releases that water, which is condensed and collected. The natural temperature swing does the work, which is exactly what makes it suited to a remote, resource-limited community.

The research plan

The plan we co-authored lays out the feasibility of a zeolite collector array scaled to Abalessa’s daily drinking-water needs. It covers the site’s climate (temperature range and relative-humidity data), estimated daily yield per unit area of zeolite, the logistics of manufacturing and deploying collector arrays, and how the system would be integrated with and maintained by the community.

Getting there meant synthesising sources across materials science, humanitarian engineering, regional geography and development economics, and then writing it up so a non-specialist reader could follow the argument.

Outcome

The plan was completed and presented as part of the seminar. It was my first formal exposure to applying engineering thinking to a humanitarian problem, and it set the pattern I still follow: pick the technology that fits the constraints, check the numbers, and communicate clearly.

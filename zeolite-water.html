Water from air, Abalessa

A research plan for a solar-driven zeolite water harvester serving a neighbourhood in the Algerian Sahara: air in at night, drinking water out by day, gravity-fed from a hill to a week’s storage. No grid, no pump, no refrigeration.

The problem

Abalessa is a town of about 9,000 people in the Tamanrasset Province of southern Algeria, deep in the Sahara. ThinkHazard rates its water scarcity as a high-level hazard: drought is expected roughly every five years, and the country as a whole is drawing 38% more groundwater than the natural recharge rate. What water there is often isn’t safe. Fertiliser runoff pushes nitrates past World Health Organization limits, and regional sediments carry zinc, cadmium and lead. Only a small fraction of local well water is considered drinkable, and fetching it means walking, sometimes through the dust storms that hit weekly in spring.

WPI’s Great Problems Seminar puts first-year teams on problems like this one. Ours asked: what could a community here run itself, with no grid, that produces water clean enough to drink straight away?

The technology

The plan is built on a dual-stage atmospheric water harvester developed at MIT by Alina LaPotin, Evelyn Wang and colleagues. Zeolite is a microporous aluminosilicate that adsorbs water vapour onto its enormous internal surface, holding it on the surface rather than inside the material, which is what makes it easy to release again. At night, air is drawn through layers of zeolite and the material loads up with moisture. By day the box seals itself, a solar-absorber plate warms the top layer, and the water comes off as vapour and condenses on a copper plate above the next layer down.

The part that makes it efficient is the cascade: the heat released when vapour condenses on one plate is what drives desorption from the layer beneath it, so the device heats and cools its stages in a chain rather than one at a time. The whole thing runs on sunlight and the day-night temperature swing. MIT measured about 0.8 litres per square metre per day and operation down to roughly 20% relative humidity. Abalessa sits near 9%, so our sizing treated 0.8 L/m² as an optimistic ceiling and flagged the humidity gap as the biggest open risk.

Sizing the system

The town is too large for a first deployment, so the plan sizes a pilot for one neighbourhood of about 100 people. At 6.4 litres per person per day for drinking and cooking, that’s 640 litres a day, which at 0.8 L/m² means 800 square metres of zeolite. Arranged as MIT’s five-layer stacks, that becomes ten 4 m × 4 m machines with a 160 m² solar-absorber footprint. Condensing 640 litres takes about 1.45 million kilojoules a day, which over Algeria’s 13 hours of daylight works out to roughly 31 kW of solar input across the array. The sun supplies it; the machines themselves need almost no power.

Site, pipes and storage

Most of Abalessa sits on flat ground, but there is a hill about 54 metres above the nearest houses with a 40 m × 80 m top, room for the machines several times over. The plan puts the collectors on the summit and a water tower 38 metres above the houses on the slope, so the zeolite sits 16 metres above the tank. A Reynolds-number and Moody-chart check on a 6 cm PVC main gives 9.5 metres of friction loss over the 52-metre run, less than the 16-metre drop, so gravity alone moves the water and no pump is needed. Machines are spread out so they don’t shadow each other’s airflow, with a 215-metre network of feeder pipes.

Storage is sized to a full week rather than a municipal day, because the harvester only produces during daylight and its yield is uncertain: 4,480 litres, a 4.5 m³ welded galvanised-steel cylinder proportioned for minimum surface area. Distribution to 22 households runs through 1½-inch pipe, sized for 70% of every faucet running at once at the US 2.2 gallon-per-minute limit.

Filtration

Water harvested from air starts far cleaner than groundwater, and zeolite’s polarity gives it a second job as an ion-exchange filter that pulls zinc and lead out of solution. For a final barrier the plan specifies “super sand”: ordinary sand coated with graphene oxide, which in Hou et al.’s tests took mercury from 400 parts per billion to under 1 in a little over half an hour.

Cost and what it would take

Priced from off-the-shelf listings, the array comes to roughly $2.8 million, and almost all of it is the zeolite at laboratory-membrane prices. The pipes, copper plating and timber together are under $11,000. That number is the honest headline of the plan: the technology is sound and the civil engineering is cheap, but a real deployment depends on bulk zeolite. Algeria happens to be rich in palygorskite clay that can be hydrothermally converted to zeolite LTA, which points to a local supply chain and a manufacturing industry rather than an import.

The plan also traces the community side: unskilled work digging trenches and landscaping for a region with about 30% youth unemployment, skilled welding and plumbing for Tamanrasset businesses, and ongoing monitoring that the University of Tamanrasset, three kilometres away, could staff through co-ops.

What I took from it

This was my first engineering problem with no answer key. We had to pick a technology, find its real numbers in the literature, and then do the sizing, the fluid mechanics and the costing ourselves, and be honest about where the numbers didn’t close. The habit of running the calculation and stating the caveat, rather than hand-waving either one, is the same one I bring to everything on this site.

LinkedIn+

LinkedIn shows you one profile at a time. I wanted to see the whole network: who moved where, which companies cluster, and which connections are real relationships rather than inferred ones. So I built a pipeline that maintains that picture for me every day.

What it does

LinkedIn+ is a private, self-hosted “network intelligence” tool for one account: mine. Each day it pulls a fresh copy of my connections through LinkedIn’s own data-export feature, notices what changed, enriches the people and companies it doesn’t know yet, and rebuilds a graph I can explore in a browser. It’s a personal instrument, not a product, and it lives entirely on my machine.

How it runs unattended

ExportA browser session requests the LinkedIn data archive.
WatchAn IMAP watcher spots the “your archive is ready” email.
DiffThe new CSV is compared with the last snapshot; only changes move on.
EnrichExperience, education, skills and company pages fill in the gaps, slowly.
PublishExcel is the master store; a JSON export feeds the graph.

The three parts live in three separate folders on purpose: orchestration, enrichment, and data. That split is a hard rule in the codebase, and it’s what let me rebuild the enricher from scratch in August without touching a single row of data.

The graph

The front-end is a static page fed by network.json. It has five views (network, company, industry, school, discipline) in 2D and 3D, with company clustering and an org-chart layout driven by seniority. Two classifiers I wrote from scratch make those views possible: a 17-tier discipline-by-seniority title taxonomy that turns free-text job titles into something sortable, and a four-tier Greek-organization detector covering 68 organizations and roughly 8,400 chapter-name variants.

The biggest upgrade was a mutual-connections scan. Before it, “these two people know each other” was inferred from shared schools and employers. After it, the graph carries 15,920 ground-truth relationship edges across 155 scanned profiles.

The bug that took a month

In July 2026 LinkedIn shipped a server-driven UI redesign. Nothing errored; the pipeline simply started writing empty fields, and I didn’t notice until the diffs went quiet. Fixing it meant reverse-engineering the new page structure, writing a new selector strategy, and building a 46-profile regression corpus so I could prove the rebuilt extractor matched the old one byte for byte before trusting it again.

Data handling

The core of the pipeline is LinkedIn’s own export of my own data, requested the way any member can. Profile enrichment reads pages I can already see when logged in, runs slowly and in the background, and is documented in the codebase as a separate, higher-risk path with its own rules. When a shortcut came up that would have meant working around LinkedIn’s limits with a second account, I evaluated it and rejected it. Nothing about other people leaves my machine: the public repository holds the code and a visualization, not the workbook.

Stirling engine, CAM to CNC

A working Stirling engine, machined from raw stock. Solid model in, toolpaths out, then the CNC lathe and mill, the measuring tools, and finally an assembly that runs on the heat of a hand.

Overview

ME 1800 is where WPI mechanical engineers first make something they designed on a screen exist in metal. The project was a small Stirling engine: a displacer cylinder, a power piston, a crank and a flywheel, every part cut from bar and plate stock on the shop’s CNC machines.

From model to toolpath

Each part started as a solid model and went through Mastercam 2025 to plan the toolpaths: which operations, in which order, with which tools and work-holding, so the part could be finished in as few setups as possible without losing the datums between them. Simulating the paths before cutting caught the collisions and air-cuts that would otherwise cost stock and time.

On the machines

Turned parts, the cylinders and piston, ran on the CNC lathe; the plates and brackets ran on the mill. The habit that mattered most was measuring: checking bores and shafts against the drawing with calipers and micrometers after each operation, and looking at surface finish, because a Stirling engine only runs when the piston seals well enough to hold a pressure difference while still moving freely.

Outcome

The engine assembled and ran. More than the part itself, the course gave me a working feel for the whole chain from CAD to CAM to a finished part, and for the tolerances that decide whether an assembly works or just fits. It’s the same instinct I now bring to software: plan the operation, check the measurement, and don’t call it done until it moves.
